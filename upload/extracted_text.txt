CHAPTER ONE

Introduction / Background 

Domestic services have existed for as long as human societies have
formed households and social hierarchies. In early civilizations,
domestic work was closely linked to systems of slavery and servitude. In
ancient societies such as those of Egypt, Greece, and Rome, household
tasks like cooking, cleaning, and childcare were typically performed by
enslaved people or servants, and the presence of domestic workers was a
clear sign of wealth and social status.

During the medieval period, domestic service became a common feature of
everyday life, especially in feudal societies. Servants often lived with
their employers and were considered part of the household rather than
separate workers. Many young people entered domestic service temporarily
as a way to gain food, shelter, skills, and social connections. At this
time, domestic work was not viewed as a permanent occupation but as a
stage in life before marriage or independent work. The early modern
period saw domestic service expand further with urban growth and
economic change. As towns and cities developed, more households employed
servants, and domestic service became one of the largest forms of
employment, particularly for women. Although servants increasingly
received wages, relationships between employers and workers remained
deeply unequal and shaped by rigid social hierarchies.

The Industrial Revolution brought major changes to labor and household
life. While many workers moved into factories, domestic service
continued to thrive, especially among the growing middle classes. Hiring
domestic workers became a symbol of respectability, and domestic labor
was more clearly defined as paid work. However, it remained poorly
regulated, with long hours, low wages, and limited rights, and it
continued to be dominated by women.

In the twentieth century, the demand for live-in domestic workers
declined in many parts of the world due to labor reforms, expanded
education, and the introduction of household technologies that reduced
the need for manual labor. Nevertheless, domestic services did not
disappear. Instead, they adapted, increasingly relying on part-time,
informal, and migrant labor, often without adequate legal protection.

Today, domestic services remain essential to households across the
globe, particularly in areas such as childcare, elder care, and
housekeeping. Although there is growing recognition of domestic work as
valuable and legitimate labor, it continues to reflect long-standing
issues of inequality related to gender, class, and migration. The
history of domestic services therefore reveals both continuity and
change, showing how essential household labor has evolved while
remaining deeply connected to broader social and economic structures.

The rapid advancement of digital technologies has transformed how
services are delivered across various industries. Domestic
services—including cleaning, laundry, childcare, cooking, home repairs,
and caregiving—remain essential to households but are still
predominantly managed through informal, unstructured channels. This
often results in inefficiencies such as unreliable service provision,
lack of transparency, pricing inconsistencies, and challenges in
matching service providers with clients who need specific skills.

Domestic services refer to a range of household-related activities
carried out to support the daily functioning, comfort, and well-being of
individuals or families within a home. These services include tasks such
as cleaning, cooking, laundry, childcare, elder care, gardening, and
basic household maintenance. Domestic services may be performed by paid
domestic workers, either on a full-time, part-time, or casual basis, or
by unpaid household members. Historically associated with servitude and
gendered labor, domestic services remain essential in modern societies,
particularly as families balance work and caregiving responsibilities.
They play a crucial role in maintaining healthy, organized, and
functional household environments.

The concept of a virtual space for domestic service involves creating an
online platform or digital environment where domestic service providers
and users can interact seamlessly. Such a system can offer features like
service listings, provider profiles, real-time bookings, secure payment
systems, and customer reviews. A well-developed virtual environment can
also promote trust, increase accessibility, and formalize the domestic
service sector.

Given the growing reliance on digital platforms and the increasing need
for organized access to domestic assistance, developing a virtual space
for domestic services is timely and relevant.

Problem Statement

The provision of domestic services is largely managed through informal and manual processes, resulting in inefficiencies, lack of scalability, and limited use of digital technologies. Here are some existing problems:

1.  Existing domestic service platforms often suffer from:

    -   Poor system integration of service discovery, booking,
        communication, and payment modules.

    -   Limited scalability and performance under increasing user loads.

    -   Inadequate security mechanisms for user authentication, data
        protection, and transactions.

    -   Insufficient trust and verification models for service
        providers.

2.  Users experience technical challenges such as:

    -   Inefficient search and matching algorithms.

    -   Lack of real-time scheduling and notification systems.

    -   Poorly designed user interfaces and limited accessibility.

3.  Domestic service providers lack access to:

    -   Digital systems for profile management and service availability.

    -   Automated job matching and scheduling tools.

    -   Transparent and traceable payment processing systems.

4.  From a system perspective, there is no unified, robust software
    solution that:

    -   Integrates frontend, backend, and database systems effectively.

    -   Supports secure, real-time interactions between users and
        service providers.

    -   Ensures data privacy, reliability, and fault tolerance.

    -   Allows for future extensibility and maintenance.

E. Methodology

The development of a virtual space for domestic services adopts a
user-centered, iterative methodology that integrates empirical research
with structured software development practices. The process begins with
a needs assessment phase aimed at understanding the expectations,
challenges, and interactions of both service users and domestic service
providers. Data is gathered through surveys, interviews, and focus group
discussions, complemented by a review of existing digital domestic
service platforms. The insights obtained are translated into functional
and non-functional system requirements, including service discovery and
booking, real-time communication, secure payment processing, user
verification, privacy protection, accessibility, and system reliability.

The system implementation follows an agile development methodology,
enabling incremental development and continuous feedback. The front end
of the virtual space is developed using HTML5, CSS3, and JavaScript,for
responsive and dynamic user interfaces. The backend system is
implemented using Node.js with Express which manage business logic,
service scheduling, user authentication, and communication between
system components. Data is stored and managed using a relational
database such as MySQL for scalability and data structure requirements.

Additional software components are integrated to enhance platform
functionality and trust. Firebase is used for secure user authentication
and role-based access control, while Stripe or Paystack APIs are
employed for secure payment processing. Cloud services such as Google
Cloud, or Hostinger support hosting, storage, and scalability of the
virtual space. Version control and collaboration are managed using Git
and GitHub, and continuous integration tools are used to ensure stable
system updates.

Testing and evaluation are conducted throughout development using
software testing tools such as Jest, Selenium, or Postman to verify
functionality, performance, and security. Usability testing is carried
out with end users to evaluate system efficiency, effectiveness, and
satisfaction. Feedback from this stage informs iterative refinements
prior to deployment.

Throughout the development lifecycle, ethical and regulatory
considerations are addressed through secure data handling practices,
compliance with data protection regulations, and transparent
representation of domestic service providers. This methodology ensures
that the virtual space is not only technically robust but also socially
responsible, scalable, and responsive to the evolving needs of domestic
service users and providers.

2.1 Theoretical Framework 

A theoretical framework provides the conceptual basis upon which the
system is designed and implemented. This project is guided by a
combination of the Systems Theory, Information Systems Theory, and the
Technology Acceptance Model (TAM).

The development of a virtual space for domestic services is grounded in
theories from information systems, service management, and technology
adoption. This study is anchored primarily on the Technology Acceptance
Model (TAM), Platform Economy Theory, and Service Quality Theory, which
together explain how digital platforms are designed, adopted, and
utilized in service-based contexts.

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) posits that users’ acceptance and
use of a technological system are determined by perceived usefulness and
perceived ease of use (Davis, 1989). In the context of a virtual space
for domestic services, perceived usefulness refers to the extent to
which users believe the platform enhances efficiency in finding,
booking, and managing domestic services, while perceived ease of use
relates to how simple and intuitive the platform interface is. These
factors influence users’ intention to adopt and continuously use the
virtual space.

The Platform Economy Theory provides a foundation for understanding
virtual spaces as digital intermediaries that facilitate interactions
between service providers (domestic workers) and service consumers
(households). This theory emphasizes network effects, trust mechanisms,
and value co-creation. As more users and service providers participate
in the platform, the value of the virtual space increases, thereby
improving accessibility, market efficiency, and service availability.
The platform’s design features—such as user profiles, rating systems,
and secure payment channels—play a critical role in enabling these
interactions.

Additionally, Service Quality Theory explains how users evaluate
services delivered through digital platforms. Dimensions such as
reliability, responsiveness, assurance, and empathy are adapted to the
virtual environment through features like timely service matching,
transparent communication, verified credentials, and feedback systems.
High perceived service quality enhances user satisfaction and trust,
which are essential for sustained platform usage.

Integrating these theories, the framework proposes that the development
of a virtual space for domestic services (independent variable),
characterized by system functionality, usability, security, and service
features, influences user acceptance, trust, and satisfaction (mediating
variables). These, in turn, affect effective utilization and
sustainability of the platform (dependent variable). External factors
such as digital literacy, internet accessibility, and regulatory
environment may further moderate these relationships.Overall, this
theoretical framework provides a structured explanation of how
technological design, platform dynamics, and service quality principles
collectively shape the successful development and adoption of a virtual
space for domestic services.

2.2.1 Virtual Space For Domestic Services

A Domestic Service Virtual Space is an integrated, software-driven
system designed to support the coordination, administration, and
financial management of domestic service transactions within a digitally
mediated service ecosystem. Core functional modules of the platform
include service discovery and matching, booking and scheduling, service
provider profiling and verification, pricing and billing management,
transaction processing, performance monitoring, and analytics-based
reporting. When integrated with Paystack digital payment system, the
platform facilitates secure, cashless financial transactions through
online payments, bank transfers, and bank deposit tellers, ensuring
end-to-end transaction traceability.

Digital payment systems have achieved widespread adoption due to their
high transaction speed, operational convenience, transparency, and
enhanced security infrastructure. Within the domestic services context,
digital payments significantly reduce risks associated with cash-based
transactions, including theft, misappropriation, and payment disputes.
The system enables households to execute remote payments upon service
fulfillment, while service providers and platform administrators gain
real-time visibility into transaction status, payment confirmation, and
revenue flows.

The integration of service management functionalities with digital
payment infrastructure results in a centralized, interoperable platform
in which service, financial, and user data are automatically
synchronized upon transaction completion. This real-time data
integration enhances accountability among platform participants,
supports auditability, and improves operational efficiency through
automated reconciliation and reporting processes. Furthermore, the
availability of structured transactional data enables platform operators
to implement data-driven decision-making related to service
optimization, pricing strategies, demand forecasting, and workforce
management.

The proposed system operationalizes these principles through a scalable,
web-based architecture designed to accommodate increasing user demand,
heterogeneous service types, and evolving regulatory requirements. By
combining digital service coordination with secure financial processing,
the platform establishes a robust technological foundation for
efficiency, trust, and sustainability in the domestic services sector.

2.2.2 Web 3.0 Technology

Web 3.0 refers to the next stage in the evolution of the internet,
designed to move control away from centralized platforms and return it
to users. Unlike today’s web, where data, identity, and digital
interactions are largely owned and managed by large corporations, Web
3.0 is built on decentralized technologies that allow individuals to own
their data, digital assets, and online identities directly. The heart of
Web 3.0 is blockchain technology. A blockchain is a distributed ledger
maintained by a network of computers rather than a single central
authority. Every transaction recorded on a blockchain is transparent,
verifiable, and extremely difficult to alter. This structure makes it
possible for strangers to interact and exchange value over the internet
without relying on intermediaries such as banks, payment processors, or
platform operators.

Evolution of 3.0 Technology

To understand Web 3.0, it’s essential to first look at the previous
phases of the internet’s evolution. While these phases overlap and
influence one another, each is marked by distinct technological
advancements and shifts in how we interact with the web.

Web 1.0: The Static Web

Web 1.0, often referred to as the “static web,” was the internet in its
infancy, from roughly the early 1990s to the early 2000s. During this
time, websites were static pages that contained simple text and images.
Interaction was limited to reading content, and websites were often
designed by developers and businesses to present information in a
one-way manner.Think back to those early days of the internet:
GeoCities, AOL, or early versions of Yahoo and MSN. The web was a
static, information-driven experience. The user could only consume
content, not interact with it. Websites were like digital
brochures—helpful but passive. This era of the internet was defined by
limited interactivity, basic HTML pages, and centralized control by a
few dominant companies.

Web 2.0: The Social Web (The Interactive and Collaborative Web)

In the early 2000s, the internet began to evolve into what we now call
Web 2.0. This era is marked by the rise of social media platforms,
user-generated content, and interactive web applications. The web became
more dynamic, and users began to actively contribute content, making it
far more interactive. Web 2.0 introduced the concept of the “social
web,” where platforms like Facebook, Twitter, YouTube, and later
Instagram and TikTok, allowed users to share their thoughts, images, and
videos with others. This created a highly collaborative environment,
where the boundaries between producers and consumers of content became
increasingly blurred. E-commerce also flourished during the Web 2.0 era.
Companies like Amazon, eBay, and Shopify revolutionized how goods and
services were bought and sold online, creating massive online
marketplaces. Google and other search engines became central to
discovering information, and cloud computing allowed for a more
distributed and scalable internet infrastructure. However,despite all
these advancements, Web 2.0 still had its limitations. The internet
remained largely centralized, controlled by large corporations like
Google, Facebook, and Amazon, which dominated the online experience.
These companies collected vast amounts of user data, which often raised
concerns about privacy and control. Additionally, users were not truly
in control of their data—companies owned the platforms and the
information users shared.

Web 3.0: The Decentralized Web (The Future of the Internet)

Unlike its predecessors, Web 3.0 is not just an iteration of the
internet; it is a complete rethinking of how the internet operates. Web
3.0 promises to move away from the centralized, corporate-controlled
model of Web 2.0 and instead emphasize decentralization, user
empowerment, and enhanced privacy. Web 3.0 leverages a range of
cutting-edge technologies—blockchain, artificial intelligence, machine
learning, and decentralized networks—to create an internet that is more
open, secure, and user-centric. It envisions a world where users have
control over their own data, where online identities are decentralized,
and where interactions are more seamless and personalized. Web 3.0 is
about decentralization, ownership, and privacy. This new version of the
internet is designed to empower users rather than corporations, shifting
the balance of power and enabling a more equitable online environment.

Key Features of Web 3.0

Web 3.0 is built on a few core principles and technologies that
differentiate it from Web 2.0. Let’s explore some of these key features.

1. Decentralization

One of the most significant changes in Web 3.0 is the move toward
decentralization. In Web 2.0, a small number of centralized entities
control the majority of online services. Companies like Google,
Facebook, and Amazon control vast amounts of user data and shape the
online experience. Web 3.0 seeks to change this by decentralizing
control over data, applications, and online experiences. Blockchain
technology is at the heart of this transformation. By using distributed
ledgers, blockchain allows for decentralized applications (dApps) to
operate without the need for a central authority. These dApps can run on
peer-to-peer networks, where users can directly interact with each
other, without relying on intermediaries.The shift to decentralization
also means that users will have more control over their own data.
Instead of corporations owning and monetizing user information, users
will be able to store and manage their own data, giving them greater
privacy and autonomy.

2. Ownership of Data

In Web 3.0, the concept of data ownership is redefined. In Web 2.0, most
of our personal data is owned by corporations. Social media platforms,
e-commerce sites, and search engines collect data about us to target ads
and improve services. However, this data is often used in ways that
benefit companies, not the users.Web 3.0 introduces the concept of
personal data sovereignty, where users have full control over their
data. By leveraging blockchain technology, individuals can securely
store their data and share it only with those they trust. This allows
for greater privacy, transparency, and security. Users can also
potentially monetize their data, creating new economic opportunities.

3. Smart Contracts and Automation

Another key feature of Web 3.0 is the use of smart contracts. Smart
contracts are self-executing contracts with the terms of the agreement
directly written into code. These contracts automatically execute when
predefined conditions are met, eliminating the need for intermediaries
such as lawyers or brokers. Smart contracts are powered by blockchain
technology and are crucial to the development of decentralized
applications (dApps). They can be used in a variety of industries, from
finance and real estate to supply chain management and gaming. By
automating processes and removing middlemen, smart contracts can reduce
costs, increase efficiency, and create new business models.

4. Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning

Web 3.0 will also see the integration of artificial intelligence (AI)
and machine learning (ML) to create smarter, more personalized online
experiences. These technologies allow for the analysis of vast amounts
of data in real time, enabling the internet to understand user
preferences, behaviors, and needs more accurately.With AI and ML, Web
3.0 applications can anticipate user actions and deliver personalized
content, recommendations, and services. Whether it’s personalized
newsfeeds, tailored product recommendations, or automated customer
service, AI-powered systems will create a more intuitive and engaging
internet experience.

5. Semantic Web and Better Search

Web 3.0 will also feature an evolution of search engines, enabled by the
“semantic web.” In Web 2.0, search engines like Google rely on
keyword-based algorithms to index and rank content. While these
algorithms are effective, they often struggle to understand the true
meaning behind a search query.The semantic web aims to improve this by
allowing machines to understand the context and relationships between
data. This would enable more accurate search results and richer
interactions with digital content. Instead of simply matching keywords,
the semantic web will enable more meaningful connections between
information, making it easier for users to find exactly what they are
looking for.

The Impact of Web 3.0 Across Industries

Web 3.0 has the potential to transform virtually every sector of
society, from finance and healthcare to entertainment and governance.
Below, we explore some of the industries that will be most impacted by
this next generation of the internet.

1. Finance: Decentralized Finance (DeFi)

The rise of Web 3.0 coincides with the rise of decentralized finance
(DeFi). DeFi refers to financial services that are built on blockchain
technology, offering users access to lending, borrowing, trading, and
other financial services without the need for traditional banks or
financial intermediaries.By using smart contracts and decentralized
platforms, DeFi allows users to have full control over their financial
transactions and assets. This opens up new opportunities for individuals
who may not have access to traditional banking services, particularly in
developing countries.

2. Healthcare: Secure and Decentralized Health Data

In healthcare, Web 3.0 promises to revolutionize how patient data is
managed. Instead of relying on centralized healthcare institutions to
store sensitive medical records, blockchain technology could enable
patients to control their health data securely. This would ensure that
medical records are tamper-proof, while also giving patients more
ownership over their health information.Furthermore, decentralized
healthcare platforms could allow for more efficient and transparent
clinical trials, better supply chain management for pharmaceuticals, and
improved accessibility to medical services globally.

3. Entertainment: Tokenized Content and NFTs

The entertainment industry is also being transformed by Web 3.0,
particularly through the rise of non-fungible tokens (NFTs) and
tokenized content. Web 3.0 offers creators new ways to monetize their
work through NFTs, allowing them to sell digital art, music, videos, and
other forms of content directly to consumers, without intermediaries.
This shift could change the power dynamics of the entertainment
industry, enabling smaller creators to reach audiences directly and
retain more control over their intellectual property. The proliferation
of decentralized platforms could also enable new forms of content
distribution, changing the way we consume media.

4. Governance: Decentralized Governance and DAOs

Web 3.0 introduces the possibility of decentralized governance through
decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs). DAOs are organizations
run by smart contracts, where decisions are made collectively by
stakeholders rather than a centralized leadership. In the context of Web
3.0, DAOs could enable more democratic, transparent, and inclusive forms
of governance. Whether in the corporate world, political systems, or
community organizations, DAOs could empower individuals to have a more
direct say in how decisions are made, creating a more participatory and
open system.

2.2.3 Database 

A database is an organized collection of data that is stored
electronically and designed to be easily accessed, managed, and updated.
Databases are used to store large amounts of information in a structured
way so that data can be efficiently retrieved, inserted, modified, and
deleted. They form the backbone of most modern software systems,
including websites, mobile applications, banking systems, and enterprise
platforms.

Components of a Database

A database is not merely a collection of stored data; it is a structured
system composed of multiple interdependent components that work together
to ensure efficient storage, retrieval, manipulation, and protection of
information. These components collectively enable databases to support
modern information systems across various domains. At the core of every
database is data, which represents the raw facts and figures stored
within the system. Data may include textual information such as names
and descriptions, numerical values such as prices and scores, multimedia
content such as images, audio, and videos, or even complex data such as
documents and sensor readings. This data typically represents real-world
entities like users, products, transactions, or events. The value of a
database lies in how well this data is structured and managed to produce
meaningful information.

Closely related to data is the database schema, which serves as the
logical blueprint of the database. The schema defines how data is
organized, specifying tables, attributes, data types, constraints, and
relationships between different entities. By enforcing a predefined
structure, the schema ensures data consistency and integrity. It also
allows developers and database administrators to understand the
organization of data without needing to inspect individual records.
Changes to the schema are carefully managed because they affect how
applications interact with the database.

The Database Management System (DBMS) is the software layer that
controls and manages the database. It acts as an interface between the
database and its users or applications. The DBMS is responsible for
storing data on physical storage devices, processing queries, managing
transactions, enforcing security rules, and maintaining data integrity.
It also provides mechanisms for backup, recovery, and concurrency
control. Examples of widely used DBMSs include MySQL, PostgreSQL, Oracle
Database, Microsoft SQL Server, and MongoDB. Without a DBMS, managing
large volumes of data efficiently and securely would be impractical.

Another critical component is queries, which are formal instructions
used to interact with the database. Queries allow users to retrieve
specific data, insert new records, update existing information, or
delete unwanted data. In relational databases, queries are typically
written in Structured Query Language (SQL), a standardized language
designed for database operations. Queries are central to transforming
stored data into useful information for decision-making and application
functionality.

Finally, users represent the human or system entities that interact with
the database. Users may include database administrators who manage and
maintain the system, developers who build applications on top of the
database, and end users who access data through applications or
reporting tools. Each user is assigned specific roles and access
privileges to ensure security and prevent unauthorized data
manipulation.

 Types of Databases

Databases can be classified based on how data is structured, stored, and
accessed. The two primary categories are Relational (SQL) databases and
NoSQL databases, each designed to meet different application
requirements.

Relational databases, organize data into tables consisting of rows and
columns. Each row represents a record, while each column represents an
attribute of the entity. Relationships between tables are established
using keys, such as primary keys and foreign keys. Relational databases
rely on a strict, predefined schema, which ensures data accuracy and
consistency. One of the defining characteristics of relational databases
is their strong support for ACID properties, which guarantee reliable
transaction processing. Due to their structured nature and reliability,
relational databases are widely used in systems that require accuracy,
consistency, and integrity, such as banking, payroll, and inventory
management systems. Common examples include MySQL, PostgreSQL, Oracle
Database, and Microsoft SQL Server.

NoSQL databases are designed to handle large volumes of unstructured or
semi-structured data. The term “NoSQL” means “Not Only SQL,” indicating
that these databases may support query languages other than SQL and do
not follow the rigid table-based structure of relational databases.
NoSQL databases emphasize flexibility, scalability, and performance,
especially in distributed and cloud environments. NoSQL databases are
further divided into several subtypes:

Document-oriented databases store data as documents in formats such as
JSON or BSON, making them suitable for web applications and content
management systems. Key-value stores store data as simple key–value
pairs and are optimized for fast data retrieval. Wide-column databases
organize data by databases columns rather than rows, making them
efficient for large-scale analytical workloads. Graph store data as
nodes and relationships, enabling efficient handling of interconnected
data such as social networks and recommendation systems.

ACID Properties of Databases

ACID properties are fundamental principles that ensure the reliability
and correctness of database transactions. A transaction refers to a
sequence of operations performed as a single logical unit of work.

Atomicity ensures that a transaction is executed completely or not at
all. If any part of a transaction fails, the entire transaction is
rolled back, leaving the database unchanged. This prevents partial
updates that could lead to data inconsistency.

Consistency guarantees that a transaction brings the database from one
valid state to another valid state. All integrity constraints, such as
primary keys and foreign keys, must be satisfied after the transaction
is completed.

Isolation ensures that concurrent transactions do not interfere with
each other. Even when multiple transactions are executed simultaneously,
each transaction behaves as if it is executed independently.

Durability ensures that once a transaction is committed, its effects are
permanently stored in the database, even in the event of system crashes
or power failures.

2.2.4 Database Management System (DBMS) 

A Database Management System (DBMS) is software that allows users to
create, store, manage, and retrieve data efficiently and securely. It
acts as an interface between the database and users, ensuring that data
is organized, consistent, and protected from unauthorized access. A DBMS
provides several key functions. It supports data definition, allowing
the structure of the database such as tables, fields, and
relationships—to be defined. It handles data manipulation, enabling
users to insert, update, delete, and query data easily. The system also
enforces data integrity by applying rules and constraints that prevent
invalid or duplicate data. It controls user access through
authentication and authorization and often includes encryption to
protect sensitive data.

The components of Database Management System (DBMS)

A DBMS is a system software consisting of multiple integrated components
that deliver a consistent, managed environment for creating, accessing
and modifying data in databases. These components include the following:

Storage engine: This basic element of a DBMS is used to store data. The
DBMS must interface with a file system at the operating system (OS)
level to store data. It can use additional components to store data or
interface with the actual data at the file system level.

Database access language: The DBMS must also provide an API to access
the data, typically in the form of a database access language that can
be used to modify data but also create database objects and secure and
authorize access to the data. SQL is an example of a database access
language and encompasses several sets of commands, including data
control language for authorizing data access, data definition language
for defining database structures and data manipulation language for
reading and modifying data.

Optimization engine: A DBMS can also provide an optimization engine
that's used to parse database access language requests and turn them
into actionable commands for accessing and modifying data.

Query processor: After a query is optimized, the DBMS must provide a way
to run the query and return results.

Lock manager: This crucial component of the DBMS manages concurrent
access to the same data. Locks are required to ensure multiple users
aren't trying to modify the same data simultaneously.

Log manager: The DBMS records all changes made to data managed by the
DBMS. The record of changes is known as the log, and the log manager
component of the DBMS is used to ensure that log records are made
efficiently and accurately. The DBMS uses the log manager during
shutdown and startup to ensure data integrity, and it interfaces with
database utilities to create backups and run recoveries.

Data utilities A DBMS also provides a set of utilities for managing and
controlling database activities. Examples of database utilities include
reorganization, RUNSTATS, backup and copy, recover, integrity check,
load data, unload data and repair database.

Reporting and monitoring tools: Most DBMS is integrated with reporting
and monitoring tools to offer enhanced functionality for managing and
analyzing data. Reporting tools generate reports, whereas monitoring
tools track various database metrics, such as resource consumption and
user activity.

Types of Database Management System

Database Management Systems can be classified based on the data model
they use, the way data is stored, and the environment in which they
operate. Each type of DBMS is designed to address specific data
management needs and application requirements.

1. Hierarchical Database Management System

The Hierarchical DBMS organizes data in a tree-like structure where
records are arranged in parent–child relationships. Each parent record
can have multiple child records, but each child has only one parent.
This structure resembles an inverted tree. In a hierarchical DBMS, data
access follows a predefined path, making data retrieval fast when the
access pattern is known in advance. However, this model lacks
flexibility because changes in the data structure can require
significant modifications to applications. It is best suited for systems
with one-to-many relationships. Hierarchical DBMSs were widely used in
early database systems and are still applied in some legacy and
specialized systems. Example is the IBM Information Management System
(IMS).

2. Network Database Management System

The Network DBMS is an extension of the hierarchical model. It allows a
record to have multiple parent and child records, forming a graph-like
structure rather than a strict tree. This enables the representation of
many-to-many relationships. Network DBMSs provide greater flexibility
than hierarchical systems and reduce data redundancy. However, they are
complex to design, implement, and maintain. Users must have a deep
understanding of the database structure to retrieve data efficiently.
Network DBMSs are less commonly used today but were important in the
evolution of database technology. Examples are Integrated Data Store
(IDS), IDMS.

3. Relational Database Management System (RDBMS)

The Relational DBMS (RDBMS) is the most widely used type of DBMS today.
It organizes data into tables consisting of rows and columns. Each table
represents an entity, and relationships between tables are established
using primary keys and foreign keys. RDBMS use Structured Query Language
(SQL) for data definition, manipulation, and querying. They strictly
enforce data integrity through constraints and support ACID properties,
ensuring reliable transaction processing. Relational DBMS are highly
flexible, scalable, and suitable for applications requiring high
consistency and accuracy, such as banking, payroll, and inventory
systems. Examples are MySQL, PostgreSQL, Oracle Database, Microsoft SQL
Server.

4. Object-Oriented Database Management System (OODBMS)

An Object-Oriented DBMS stores data as objects, similar to
object-oriented programming concepts. Each object contains both data and
the methods that operate on the data. This model supports features such
as encapsulation, inheritance, and polymorphism. OODBMS are particularly
useful for applications involving complex data types, such as multimedia
systems, computer-aided design (CAD), and scientific simulations.
However, they are less popular than RDBMSs due to limited
standardization and tool support. Example is ObjectDB.

5. Object-Relational Database Management System (ORDBMS)

The Object-Relational DBMS combines features of both relational and
object-oriented databases. It extends the relational model by supporting
complex data types, user-defined types, and object-oriented features.
ORDBMS maintain the benefits of SQL and relational structures while
offering greater flexibility in handling complex data. They are commonly
used in enterprise systems that require both structured data management
and object-oriented capabilities. Examples are PostgreSQL, Oracle
Database.

6. NoSQL Database Management System

NoSQL DBMSs are designed to handle large volumes of unstructured or
semi-structured data. Unlike relational databases, they do not rely on
fixed schemas and are optimized for horizontal scalability and high
performance in distributed environments. NoSQL DBMSs are widely used in
big data applications, real-time analytics, cloud computing, and social
media platforms.NoSQL databases are further classified into four main
types:

-   Document-oriented DBMS: Store data as JSON-like documents (e.g.,
    MongoDB)

-   Key-Value DBMS: Store data as key–value pairs for fast access (e.g.,
    Redis)

-   Column-Family DBMS: Store data in columns for large-scale analytics
    (e.g., Cassandra)

-   Graph DBMS: Store nodes and relationships for connected data (e.g.,
    Neo4j)

7. Distributed Database Management System

A Distributed DBMS manages databases that are distributed across
multiple physical locations connected through a network. It provides a
unified view of data, making it appear as a single database to users.
Distributed DBMS improve data availability, fault tolerance, and
performance. However, they are complex to design and manage due to
issues such as network latency and data synchronization. Examples:
Google Spanner, Apache Cassandra.

8. Centralized Database Management System

In a Centralized DBMS, all data is stored and managed at a single
central location. While this simplifies management and security, it may
lead to performance bottlenecks and a single point of failure
.Centralized DBMSs are suitable for small-scale systems with limited
users.

9. Cloud Database Management System

A Cloud DBMS is hosted on cloud infrastructure and offers scalability,
high availability, and reduced maintenance costs. Cloud DBMSs support
modern applications such as SaaS platforms and serverless architectures.
Examples: Amazon Aurora, Google Cloud Spanner, Microsoft Azure Cosmos
DB.

What does a Database management system do?

A Database Management System manages the data. The database engine
enables data to be accessed, locked and modified and the
database schema defines the database's logical structure. These three
foundational data elements help provide concurrency, security, data
integrity and uniform data administration procedures.

The following are common functions that a DBMS performs:

Administration tasks: A DBMS supports many typical database
administration tasks, including change management, performance
monitoring and tuning, security, and backup and recovery. Most database
management systems are also responsible for automated rollbacks and
restarts as well as logging and auditing of activity in databases and
the applications that access them.

Storage: A DBMS provides efficient data storage and retrieval by
ensuring that data is stored in tables, rows and columns.

Concurrency control: In environments where multiple users access and
modify the database simultaneously, a DBMS guarantees controlled
transaction execution to prevent data corruption or inconsistency.

Centralized view: A DBMS provides a centralized view of data that
multiple users can access from multiple locations in a controlled
manner. A DBMS can limit what data end users see and how they view the
data, providing many views of a single database schema. End users and
software programs are free from having to understand where the data is
physically located or on what type of storage medium it resides because
the DBMS handles all requests.

Data manipulation: A DBMS ensures data integrity and consistency by
letting users insert, update, delete and modify data inside a database.

Data independence: A DBMS offers both logical and physical data
independence to protect users and applications from having to know where
data is stored or from being concerned about changes to the physical
structure of data. As long as programs use the application programming
interface (API) for the database that the DBMS provides, developers
won't have to modify programs just because changes have been made to the
database.

Backup and recovery: A DBMS facilitate backup and recovery options by
creating backup copies so that data can be restored to a consistent
state. This protects against data loss due to hardware failures,
software errors or other unforeseen events. In a relational database
management system (RDMS) -- the most widely used type of DBMS -- the API
is structured query language (SQL), a standard programming language for
defining, protecting and accessing data.

2.2.5 Models 

A data model refers to an abstract representation of data structures
that are used to organize and manage data in a database or information
system. It defines the relationship between the data elements
(representing real-world objects) and how they are organized, stored,
and retrieved. It can be helpful to imagine a building plan beneath the
data model, upon which the entire system will be built. Data models are
used to create databases and data warehouses, manage data for analytical
processing, and implement applications that enable users to access
information in meaningful ways.

Components of Data Models

A data model consists of several essential components that collectively
describe how data is organized and related:

Entities are the objects we want to represent in our data model and are
usually represented by a table. Examples of entities include customers,
orders, products, or categories.

Attributes appear as columns in specific tables. Examples include
product identifier and product name (for the Product) category
identifier and category name (for the Category).

Records are shown in rows in each table. A good example of a record is
the data of a specific product (identifier, product name, category)
stored in a table called Products.

Relationships define the associations between entities. This may be 1:1,
1:N or M:N. For example, one product could be related to another by a
specific product category.

Cardinality refers to the numerical relationship (1:1, 1:N and N:M)
between two entities in a database. For example, the relationship
between a specific product and category can be described with a 1:N
relationship — one category can be relevant for multiple products, but
one specific product is related by a specific category.

Primary key is a unique identifier of each record in a current table. It
refers to a column or a combination of columns that has a unique value
for each row. For the Product table the primary key is product_id, and
for the Category table it is category_id.

Foreign key is a column or a group of columns that points towards a
primary key in a different table — product_id and category_id. It
creates an association between two tables (Product and Category) that
enables information from one table (Product) to be linked and related to
information from another (Category).

Types of Data Models

There are three main types of data models: conceptual, logical, and
physical. Each has a different purpose and is used in a different stage
of data modeling:

A conceptual data model is usually created as a blueprint for a future
database system. Developed by gathering input from business
stakeholders, it provides a high-level overview of the entire system and
defines its components and rules in business terms.

A logical data model provides more detail about the entire system – its
entities, attributes, and relationships. It is used to develop a
database schema through the collaboration of data architects and
business analysts.

A physical data model is the most detailed representation of the system
and contains all of the physical structures for the technical
implementation of the database. This model is created by database
developers and includes information about data types, indexing, and
access methods.

Levels of Data Models

Data modeling is typically performed at three distinct levels:
conceptual, logical, and physical. Each level serves a specific purpose
in the database design process.

Conceptual Data Model

The conceptual data model represents data at a very high level and
focuses on understanding business requirements rather than technical
details. It identifies major entities and their relationships without
specifying data types or storage details. This level is often
represented using Entity–Relationship (ER) diagrams, which provide a
visual representation of entities and relationships. The conceptual
model is easily understood by non-technical stakeholders and is crucial
during requirement analysis.

Logical Data Model

The logical data model provides a more detailed representation of data.
It defines entities, attributes, primary keys, foreign keys, and
relationships more precisely, but it remains independent of any specific
DBMS. At this stage, normalization techniques are applied to reduce data
redundancy and improve consistency. The logical model forms the basis
for creating the actual database schema.

Physical Data Model

The physical data model describes how data is physically stored in the
database. It includes details such as table structures, indexes,
partitions, file storage methods, and access paths.This model is
DBMS-specific and focuses on performance optimization, storage
efficiency, and implementation details.

Types of Data Models

Hierarchical Data Model

The hierarchical data model organizes data in a tree-like structure with
parent–child relationships. Each child record has only one parent,
making the model suitable for one-to-many relationships. However, it
lacks flexibility and does not efficiently support complex
relationships.

Network Data Model

The network data model extends the hierarchical model by allowing
many-to-many relationships. Data is represented as records connected
through links. Although more flexible, the model is complex and
difficult to manage.

 Relational Data Model

The relational data model organizes data into tables with rows and
columns. Relationships are established using keys, and SQL is used for
querying and manipulation. This model is widely adopted due to its
simplicity, flexibility, and strong theoretical foundation.

Object-Oriented Data Model

The obj ect-oriented data model represents data as objects that include
both attributes and methods. It supports inheritance and encapsulation,
making it suitable for complex data types such as multimedia and
engineering applications.

Relational Data Model

The object-relational data model combines relational and object-oriented
concepts, enabling support for complex data types while retaining SQL
functionality.

NoSQL Data Models

NoSQL data models are designed for large-scale, distributed systems.
They include document, key–value, column-family, and graph models, each
optimized for specific use cases such as big data, social networks, and
real-time analytics.

Importance of Data Models in Database Design

Data models play a critical role in database development. They ensure
efficient database structure, reduce redundancy, and enhance data
integrity. A well-designed data model improves system performance,
scalability, and maintainability. In academics and industrial projects,
data models serve as a reference throughout the system lifecycle, from
requirement analysis to implementation and maintenance. They also help
in troubleshooting, upgrading, and integrating systems.

Data Models in Modern Technologies

In modern computing environments, data models are essential for
technologies such as cloud computing, big data analytics, artificial
intelligence, and Web 3.0 systems. Distributed databases and blockchain
systems rely on specialized data models to manage decentralized and
immutable data structures. Data model also play a vital role in data
science, where structured and semi-structured data must be organized
efficiently for analysis and machine learning.

2.2.6 Data Modelling

Data modeling refers to the process of creating a visual and logical representation of data requirements and structures within an information system. It defines how data elements relate to one another and how they will be stored and accessed in a database. The process serves as a blueprint for database designers, developers, and stakeholders. Data modeling answers key questions such as:

-   What data needs to be stored?

-   How is the data related?

-   What rules govern the data?

1.  Clear Definition of Data Requirements
    Data modeling helps in identifying and documenting all data elements
    required by a system, ensuring no important data is omitted.

2.  Reduction of Data Redundancy
    By organizing data logically and applying normalization techniques,
    duplication of data is minimized.

3.  Improved Data Integrity and Consistency
    Constraints and rules defined in the data model help maintain
    accurate and reliable data.

4.  Efficient Data Storage and Retrieval
    A well-designed model supports optimized database performance and
    faster query execution.

5.  Ease of Maintenance and Scalability
    Proper data modeling allows future system modifications and
    expansions with minimal disruption.

Components of Data Modeling

Data modeling is built upon several core components, which together
define the structure of a database.

Entities: An entity represents a real-world object or concept about
which data is stored. Examples include Student, Customer, Employee,
ServiceProvider, and Order. Each entity corresponds to a table in a
relational database.

Attributes: Attributes are properties or characteristics that describe an entity. For example, a Student entity may have attributes such as StudentID, Name, DateOfBirth, and Department. Each attribute represents a column in a database table.

 Relationships: Relationships define how entities are connected to one another. Common relationship types include one-to-one (1:1), one-to-many (1:M), and many-to-many (M:N). For instance, a Customer can place many Orders, but each Order belongs to one Customer.

Keys: There are two types of Keys, 

-   Primary Key: A unique identifier for each entity record.

-   Foreign Key: A field that establishes a link between two entities.

Constraints: Constraints are rules that ensure data validity and integrity. Examples include NOT NULL, UNIQUE, CHECK, and referential integrity constraints.

6. Data Modeling Techniques

Several techniques are used in data modeling, depending on the type of
system and application.

Entity–Relationship (ER) Modeling: ER modeling uses entities, attributes, and relationships to visually represent data structures. It is widely used in relational database design.

Normalization: Normalization is the process of organizing data to eliminate redundancy and dependency. It involves dividing large tables into smaller ones and defining relationships among them.

UML Class Diagrams: UML diagrams are used in object-oriented systems to represent classes, attributes, methods, and relationships.

Dimensional Modeling: Dimensional modeling is commonly used in data warehouses and business intelligence systems. It uses fact tables and dimension tables to support analytical queries.

Steps in Data Modeling

The data modeling process follows a systematic sequence of steps:

1.  Requirement analysis and data collection

2.  Identification of entities and attributes

3.  Definition of relationships and cardinalities

4.  Assignment of primary and foreign keys

5.  Application of normalization rules

6.  Development of the physical database schema

Importance of Data Modeling in System Development

Data modeling is essential in system development for several reasons. It
enhances communication between developers and stakeholders, reduces
development time, minimizes errors, and ensures the database supports
business objectives effectively. In final-year projects, a
well-documented data model demonstrates a clear understanding of system
design principles and database management concepts.

2.2.7. Software process models

A software process model is a structured framework that defines the
sequence of activities involved in the development, deployment, and
maintenance of software systems. It provides a systematic approach for
organizing software development tasks, ensuring consistency, quality,
and efficient project management. Software process models guide
developers in planning, executing, and controlling software projects by
clearly outlining phases, roles, deliverables, and workflows. A software
process model is an abstraction of the software development process. The
models specify the stages and order of a process.

A model will defined the following:

-   The tasks to be performed.

-   The input and output of each task.

-   The pre and post-conditions for each task.

-   The flow and sequence of each task.

-   The goal of a software process model is to provide guidance for
    controlling and coordinating the tasks to achieve the end product
    and objectives as effectively as possible.

System Development Life Cycle (SDLC)

System Development Life Cycle (SDLC) is a systematic framework used to
develop information systems in an organized and efficient manner. It
provides a step-by-step approach that guides system developers from the
initial idea through implementation and long-term maintenance. The SDLC
ensures that systems are developed on time, within budget, and in line
with user requirements.

Phases of System Development Life cycle (SDLC)

The System Development Life Cycle (SDLC) consists of several
interconnected phases that provide a structured framework for developing
a system. These phases include Planning, Analysis, Design, Development,
Testing, Implementation, and Maintenance. Each phase plays a vital role
in ensuring the system is successfully developed, with System Design
being especially critical in shaping the final product.

-   Planning
    The planning stage marks the beginning of the System Development
    Life Cycle and focuses on defining the purpose and scope of the
    proposed system. During this stage, organizations identify the
    problems to be solved, set project objectives, and determine the
    resources required for development. Feasibility studies are
    conducted to evaluate whether the project is technically achievable,
    economically viable, operationally practical, and feasible within
    the available time frame. Effective planning provides a clear
    direction for the project and reduces the risk of failure in later
    stages.

-   System Analysis
    System analysis involves a detailed examination of the existing
    system and the identification of user requirements for the new
    system. At this stage, developers interact with stakeholders to
    understand their needs, expectations, and challenges. Various data
    collection techniques such as interviews, questionnaires, and
    observations are used to gather accurate information. The goal of
    system analysis is to define what the system should accomplish
    without considering how it will be implemented. The findings are
    documented in a Software Requirements Specification, which serves as
    a foundation for system design.

-   System Design
    The system design stage translates the requirements identified
    during analysis into a structured blueprint for system development.
    It specifies the system architecture, database structure, user
    interfaces, input and output designs, and security mechanisms.
    Design decisions are made to ensure the system is efficient,
    reliable, scalable, and user-friendly. This stage provides
    developers with a clear guide on how the system components will be
    constructed and integrated.

-   System Development (Implementation)
    System development is the phase where the actual construction of the
    system takes place. Programmers write and compile source code based
    on the design specifications using appropriate programming languages
    and development tools. Individual modules are developed and
    integrated to form a complete system. Proper documentation is
    maintained to support debugging, future modifications, and system
    maintenance.

-   System Testing
    System testing is carried out to ensure that the developed system
    performs as expected and meets all specified requirements. This
    stage involves detecting and correcting errors, bugs, and
    inconsistencies in the system. Various testing techniques, including
    unit testing, integration testing, system testing, and user
    acceptance testing, are applied to verify functionality,
    performance, and security. Testing ensures that the system is
    reliable and ready for real-world use.

-   System Deployment
    System deployment involves installing the tested system and making
    it available to end users. During this stage, data may be migrated
    from the old system, and users are trained on how to operate the new
    system effectively. Different changeover strategies such as direct,
    parallel, or phased implementation may be used depending on
    organizational needs. Deployment ensures a smooth transition from
    the old system to the new one.

-   System Maintenance
    System maintenance is the final stage of the SDLC and focuses on
    sustaining the system after deployment. It involves correcting
    errors that arise during operation, adapting the system to changing
    user requirements, enhancing performance, and improving security.
    Maintenance ensures that the system remains efficient, reliable, and
    relevant throughout its operational life.

The waterfall model is a sequential, plan driven-process where you must
plan and schedule all your activities before starting the project. Each
activity in the waterfall model is represented as a separate phase
arranged in linear order.

It has the following phases:

Requirements

Design

Implementation

Testing

Deployment

Maintenance

Each of these phases produces one or more documents that need to be
approved before the next phase begins. However, in practice, these
phases are very likely to overlap and may feed information to one
another. The waterfall model is easy to understand and follow. It
doesn’t require a lot of customer involvement after the specification is
done. Since it’s inflexible, it can’t adapt to changes. There is no way
to see or try the software until the last phase.

The waterfall model has a rigid structure, so it should be used in cases
where the requirements are understood completely and unlikely to
radically change.

You develop the concept in the first few cycles, and then it evolves
into an implementation. Though this model is great for managing
uncertainty, it can be difficult to have stable documentation. The
spiral model can be used for projects with unclear needs or projects
still in research and development.

Agile model

The agile process model encourages continuous iterations of
development and testing. Each incremental part is developed over an
iteration, and each iteration is designed to be small and manageable so
it can be completed within a few weeks. Each iteration focuses on
implementing a small set of features completely. It involves customers
in the development process and minimizes documentation by using informal
communication.

Agile development considers the following:

Requirements are assumed to change

The system evolves over a series of short iterations

Customers are involved during each iteration

Documentation is done only when needed

Though agile provides a very realistic approach to software development,
it isn’t great for complex projects. It can also present challenges
during transfers as there is very little documentation. Agile is great
for projects with changing requirements.

2.2.8 Software Hosting

Software hosting is a fundamental concept in modern software engineering
that refers to the deployment, execution, and management of software
applications on remote computing infrastructure. These infrastructures
are typically maintained by third-party service providers and accessed
through the internet or private networks. Software hosting eliminates
the dependency on local machines for application execution and enables
centralized control, improved scalability, high availability, and
efficient maintenance.In contemporary system development, software
hosting is closely integrated into system design and the Software
Development Life Cycle (SDLC). Hosting decisions influence system
architecture, performance, security, scalability, and cost. As
organizations increasingly adopt web-based, cloud-native, and
distributed systems, software hosting has become a critical design
consideration rather than a post-development activity.

Software Hosting in the Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC)

Software hosting plays a vital role across multiple stages of the SDLC,
particularly from system design to deployment and maintenance.

During the planning and requirement analysis phase, hosting requirements
are identified based on system objectives, expected user base,
performance needs, security requirements, and budget constraints.
Decisions such as whether the system will be web-based, cloud-hosted, or
on-premises are made at this stage. These choices directly affect system
feasibility and cost estimation.

In the system design phase, hosting considerations shape architectural
decisions. Designers determine whether the system will use centralized
servers, distributed cloud infrastructure, microservices, or
container-based deployment. Hosting models influence database placement,
load balancing strategies, backup mechanisms, and fault tolerance
design. A poorly chosen hosting environment can lead to scalability and
performance bottlenecks.

During the implementation and testing phases, hosting platforms provide
development, staging, and testing environments. Many hosting solutions
support continuous integration and continuous deployment (CI/CD),
allowing automated testing and rapid deployment. This ensures that the
software behaves consistently across environments.

In the deployment phase, the software is hosted on production servers,
making it accessible to end users. Hosting platforms manage domain
configuration, security certificates, performance optimization, and
traffic handling.

Finally, during the maintenance phase, hosting providers support system
monitoring, updates, backups, and disaster recovery. Effective hosting
ensures long-term system reliability, availability, and adaptability to
changing requirements.

Types of Software Hosting

Web Hosting

Web hosting is a hosting model designed specifically for websites and
web applications. It enables software to be accessed through web
browsers using standard internet protocols such as HTTP and HTTPS. Web
hosting environments store application files, scripts, databases, and
media resources while handling user requests efficiently.

Shared hosting allows multiple applications to run on a single server
while sharing resources such as memory and processing power. Although
cost-effective, this model is limited in performance and scalability.
Virtual Private Server (VPS) hosting uses virtualization technology to
allocate dedicated resources within a shared physical server, offering
better control and reliability. Dedicated hosting provides exclusive
access to a physical server, ensuring maximum performance and security
but at a higher cost. Cloud-based web hosting distributes applications
across multiple servers, allowing dynamic resource allocation and
improved fault tolerance. Web hosting is commonly used for informational
websites, content management systems, and small-to-medium web
applications.

Cloud Hosting

Cloud hosting is a scalable hosting model that uses virtual servers
running on a cloud computing infrastructure. Unlike traditional hosting,
cloud hosting does not depend on a single physical server. Instead, it
draws resources from a network of servers across multiple data centers.

This hosting model supports elasticity, allowing systems to
automatically scale up or down based on demand. Cloud hosting enhances
system availability, as failures in one server do not disrupt the entire
application. It also enables geographic distribution, improving
performance for users in different locations.

Cloud hosting aligns strongly with modern system design principles such
as distributed computing, high availability, and fault tolerance. It is
widely adopted for enterprise systems, large-scale platforms, and
applications with fluctuating workloads.

 Platform as a Service (PaaS)

Platform as a Service (PaaS) provides a complete hosting environment that includes operating systems, development frameworks, databases, and deployment tools. In this model, developers focus solely on application development while the hosting provider manages infrastructure-related tasks.PaaS enhances productivity by supporting rapid application development, automated deployment, and integrated testing tools. It reduces system complexity and ensures consistency across development and production environments. PaaS is especially suitable for agile development methodologies, where frequent updates and rapid iteration are required.

Software as a Service (SaaS) Hosting

Software as a Service (SaaS) hosting delivers complete software
solutions over the internet. Users access the application via a web
interface without installing or maintaining the software locally. The
hosting provider manages infrastructure, security, data storage,
updates, and availability.SaaS hosting supports multi-user access,
centralized data management, and subscription-based usage models. It
simplifies software distribution and reduces operational costs for
users. SaaS systems are widely used in business management, education,
finance, and collaboration platforms.From an SDLC perspective, SaaS
hosting shifts much of the maintenance responsibility from users to
service providers, ensuring continuous system improvement and security.

Managed Hosting

Managed hosting involves outsourcing server management responsibilities
to a hosting provider. This includes system updates, security
management, monitoring, backups, and performance optimization. Managed
hosting reduces the technical burden on organizations and enhances
system reliability.This model is particularly beneficial for
mission-critical systems where uptime and security are essential. While
managed hosting increases operational costs, it significantly reduces
risk and administrative workload.

Container and Application Hosting

Container hosting uses containerization technologies to package
applications and their dependencies into isolated units. Containers
ensure consistency across development, testing, and production
environments. Container orchestration platforms manage scalability, load
balancing, and fault tolerance.

Application Hosting

Application hosting platforms provide optimized environments for
specific software types, such as content management systems or
e-commerce platforms. These platforms simplify deployment and improve
performance by offering pre-configured settings and security features.

Container and application hosting support modern system architectures
such as microservices and distributed systems.

Security Considerations in Software Hosting

Security is a critical concern in software hosting due to constant exposure to cyber threats. Hosting environments implement security mechanisms such as encryption, firewalls, intrusion detection systems, and access control policies. Secure hosting ensures data confidentiality, integrity, and availability. Regular backups, disaster recovery planning, and compliance with data protection regulations are essential components of secure hosting. During system design, security requirements must be integrated into hosting decisions to prevent vulnerabilities.

 Importance of Software Hosting in System Design

Software hosting significantly influences system performance, scalability, reliability, and maintainability. A well-chosen hosting model supports efficient resource utilization, seamless updates, and global accessibility. Hosting decisions impact system cost, development speed, and long-term sustainability. In modern information systems, software hosting is not an afterthought but a core architectural element that determines system success. It enables organizations to deliver reliable digital services and adapt to technological advancements.

3.1 Overview of Existing System

In the current domestic services landscape, most service coordination is
conducted through informal mechanisms such as personal contacts,
referrals, notice boards, and ad-hoc agencies. Households seeking help
for cleaning, caregiving, cooking, or maintenance typically rely on
word-of-mouth, walk-in agents, or unverified intermediaries rather than
digitally mediated platforms. This informal system often lacks
standardized matching, transparent pricing, secure payments, service
tracking, and quality assurance. As a result, users experience
inefficient service discovery, limited accountability, and variable
service quality. Additionally, domestic workers face challenges such as
inconsistent work supply, lack of visibility to potential users, and
absence of digital records for their work history and reputation.

Despite these advances, many of the existing systems remain limited in
one or more respects. Challenges include inconsistent provider
verification, inefficient, limited customization of services, and
insufficient support for digital payments or scheduling in real time.
Many platforms are also oriented toward general home services rather
than strictly domestic help, meaning that users may need to navigate
broad service categories rather than a system optimized for household
work. Furthermore, informal platforms and traditional agencies still
dominate in many regions, especially where smartphone penetration or
digital literacy is low, limiting the reach of existing digital
solutions.

In summary, while various on-demand service platforms have significantly
modernized aspects of domestic and home-related services, the existing
system still reveals gaps in seamless service discovery, equitable
provider representation, reliable matching, and user-centric design.
These limitations underscore the need for a more integrated virtual
space specifically tailored to domestic services. This proposed system
will incorporate optimized efficient scheduling, secure digital
payments, and a strong feedback and reputation framework to enhance
performance, reliability, and satisfaction for both service seekers and
providers.

3.2 Virtual Space for Domestic Services

3.2.1 Overview of The Proposed System

The proposed Virtual Space for Domestic Services is designed as a
centralized, web-based platform that facilitates seamless interaction
between service seekers and domestic service providers. Its primary
objective is to provide a reliable, secure, and user-centered
environment for managing service requests, scheduling, payments,
communication, and feedback. The system addresses the inefficiencies of
traditional domestic service sourcing, such as informal arrangements,
lack of trust, inconsistent service quality, and payment disputes.

The platform is intended for use by multiple stakeholders:

1.  Users (Service Seekers): Individuals or households seeking domestic
    services such as cleaning, cooking, caregiving, plumbing, laundry,
    or maintenance. Users require timely access to verified service
    providers, transparent service pricing, and secure payment
    mechanisms.

2.  Service Providers (Domestic Workers): Individuals or professionals
    offering domestic services. Providers require access to consistent
    job opportunities, secure payments, a mechanism to establish
    reputation, and tools to manage their service schedules.

3.  System Administrator: The administrator oversees the platform’s
    operations, including user verification, dispute resolution,
    monitoring service delivery, and generating system reports. The
    administrator ensures system security, data integrity, and
    operational compliance.

4.  External Payment Service Providers: These entities facilitate secure
    digital transactions, ensuring compliance with financial standards,
    processing payments accurately, and protecting user financial data.

The system is designed with a multi-layered architecture, incorporating
a presentation layer, a business logic layer, and a data management
layer. The presentation layer provides user interfaces for users,
providers, and administrators. The business logic layer handles
authentication, service matching, messaging, and payment processing. The
data layer maintains records of users, service providers, requests,
transactions, and feedback.

The system incorporates intelligent service matching algorithms that
evaluate service providers based on skill relevance, availability,
proximity, cost, and reputation. The workflow ensures that service
requests are matched to the most suitable providers efficiently.
Additionally, the platform integrates secure digital payment processing,
real-time communication, and feedback mechanisms to enhance
transparency, accountability, and user satisfaction.

Requirements gathering is a vital stage in system development that
focuses on identifying and understanding the needs of users and
stakeholders in order to design an effective and user-oriented system.
It ensures that the proposed system addresses real-life problems and
meets user expectations. In this study, requirements were gathered from

3.2.2.1 Principles of Interview

Interviews are an essential qualitative research method used to obtain
detailed and meaningful information from participants. When interviews
involve diverse social groups such as housewives, working-class
individuals, and bachelors, it is important to follow established
interview principles to ensure that the data collected is valid,
ethical, and reliable. The principle of purpose clarity emphasizes that
the interviewer must have a clear understanding of the objectives of the
interview before interacting with respondents. Every question asked
should directly relate to the research problem and objectives.
Participants should be informed about the purpose of the interview so
they understand why their opinions are being sought. In the case of
housewives, the interview purpose may focus on household
responsibilities and service needs; for working-class individuals, it
may center on work-life balance and time constraints; while for
bachelors, it may relate to independent living and convenience-based
service requirements.

Appropriateness: Housewives are appropriate respondents when the study
involves domestic management and household services, working-class
individuals are suitable when employment and income factors are
relevant, and bachelors are appropriate when independent living patterns
and personal service usage are being examined.

Simple and clear: When interviewing housewives, practical and familiar
terms related to home activities should be used. For working-class
respondents, questions should be direct and concise, while for
bachelors, language should be straightforward and relatable to their
lifestyle.

Neutrality and non-bias: Questions should not be leading or suggestive,
and the interviewer should avoid expressing personal opinions that may
influence the respondent’s answers. This is particularly important when
interviewing housewives about household practices, working-class
individuals about financial or work conditions, and bachelors about
lifestyle choices, as assumptions and stereotypes can distort the
accuracy of responses.

Time management: Interviews should be conducted within a reasonable
duration and scheduled at times that are convenient for participants.
Housewives may prefer interviews during less busy household periods,
working-class individuals may require interviews outside work hours, and
bachelors often have more flexible schedules. Proper time management
improves cooperation and the quality of responses.

Ethical Consideration: Participants should be informed about the nature
of the interview and give their consent before participating.
Confidentiality and anonymity must be guaranteed, and respondents should
be free to withdraw at any time without consequences. Ethical
sensitivity is especially important when discussing private family
matters with housewives, employment-related issues with working-class
individuals, and personal living arrangements with bachelors.

Flexibility: While the interviewer may have a set of prepared questions,
follow-up questions can be asked to gain deeper insights when necessary.
Flexibility allows housewives to fully explain domestic challenges,
enables working-class respondents to discuss work-related constraints in
detail, and encourages bachelors to express personal preferences and
experiences freely.

Accurate Recordings: All information provided by respondents must be
captured correctly to ensure reliable analysis. This can be done through
note-taking or audio recording, provided consent is obtained. The
interviewer should avoid altering or summarizing responses in a way that
changes their original meaning. Accurate recording ensures that the real
experiences of housewives, working-class individuals, and bachelors are
faithfully represented in the study.

Consistency: This requires that all respondents be asked the same core
questions to ensure comparability of responses across different groups.
Although minor adjustments may be made to suit each group’s context,
maintaining consistency allows the researcher to identify similarities
and differences among housewives, working-class individuals, and
bachelors in a systematic manner.

The collected data was analyzed to identify both functional and
non-functional requirements. Functional requirements include service
request submission, scheduling, payment processing, and feedback
mechanisms. Non-functional requirements include usability, reliability,
security, and system performance. These requirements were carefully
documented to guide system design and implementation.

3.5 System Design

System design is the phase of system development that translates user
requirements into a structured blueprint for building the system. It
defines how the system components interact, how data flows, and how
users communicate with the system. The design of the proposed virtual
platform for domestic services is aimed at ensuring efficiency,
usability, security, and scalability for housewives, working-class
individuals, and bachelors.

The system adopts a users–server architecture, where users interact with
the system through a web or mobile application, while all processing and
data management are handled by a centralized server. This architecture
allows multiple users to access the system simultaneously while ensuring
centralized control of data and services. End users access the system
using smartphones or computers connected to the internet, making the
platform convenient and widely accessible.

The user interface design focuses on simplicity and ease of navigation.
The system provides different user views based on roles, such as service
seekers and service providers. Users can register, log in, browse
available domestic services, submit service requests, schedule
appointments, make payments, and provide feedback. The interface is
designed to minimize complexity so that users with different educational
and technological backgrounds can interact with the system easily.

The application logic design defines how the system processes user
requests. When a user submits a service request, the system validates
the information, stores it in the database, and matches it with an
appropriate service provider. The system also handles scheduling,
notifications, payment processing, and feedback management. Business
rules are embedded in this layer to ensure proper service delivery and
prevent errors.

The database design focuses on efficient data storage and retrieval. The
database contains tables for users, service providers, services, service
requests, payments, and feedback. Relationships are established among
these tables to ensure data integrity and reduce redundancy. Proper
indexing and constraints are applied to improve performance and maintain
consistency.

Security is an important aspect of the system design. Authentication and
authorization mechanisms are implemented to ensure that only registered
users can access the system. User data and transactions are protected
using encryption and secure communication protocols. Access control
ensures that users can only perform actions permitted by their roles.

In conclusion, the system design provides a clear structure for
implementing the proposed domestic services platform. By defining the
architecture, interface, application logic, database structure, and
security mechanisms, the design ensures that the system meets user
requirements and operates efficiently. This design serves as a
foundation for system implementation and testing.

For the Virtual Space for Domestic Services, System design helps in
understanding how userss, service providers, administrators, and system
components interact within the platform, ensuring clarity,
maintainability, and alignment with functional requirements.

3.5.2 Context Diagram

A Context Diagram (also known as a Level 0 Data Flow Diagram) is the
highest-level abstraction of a system. Its primary goal is to define the
scope and boundaries of the software by showing how it interacts with
the outside world. The entire system is represented by a single process,
and the focus is entirely on the inputs and outputs between the system
and external actors.

Key Components

A standard Context Diagram uses three main symbols to represent the
environment:

-   The System (The Central Process): Represented as a single large
    circle or rounded rectangle at the center. It is labeled "0" and
    named after the system (e.g., "Virtual Space for Domestic
    Services").

-   External Entities (Terminators): Represented as squares or
    rectangles. These are the people, organizations, or other systems
    that interact with your platform but exist outside of it (e.g.,
    Userss, Banks, or Administrators).

-   Data Flows: Represented by arrows. These show the information moving
    between the entities and the system. They are labeled with the
    specific type of data being exchanged.

A Data Flow Diagram (DFD) is a graphical and logical representation that
illustrates how data moves through a system, the processes that

external entities that interact with the system. In the context of a

COMPONENTS of Data flow diagram

Major Processes in the DFD

User Registration and Authentication: This process handles the
collection and verification of user credentials for both users and
service providers. Registration data flows from the users into the
system, where it is validated and stored in the user database.
Successful authentication enables access to system services, while
failed authentication results in error notifications.

Service Request Management: In this process, users submit service
requests specifying service type, location, date, and time. The system
processes these requests and forwards them to suitable service providers
based on availability and service category. Providers respond by

status accordingly.

Administration and Reporting process enables the system administrator to

Entity–Relationship (ER) Diagram

An Entity Relationship Diagram (ERD) is a conceptual blueprint of your
database. It defines the "entities" (the things we store data about),
their "attributes" (the details of those things), and the
"relationships" (how they connect to each other).

For your Virtual Space for Domestic Services, the ERD is crucial because
it ensures that a users is correctly linked to their booking and that
the payment is tied to the right transaction.

1. The Core Entities

-   USER: This is the parent table. It stores login credentials for
    everyone. By having a central USER table, you ensure that a person
    doesn't have to register twice if they want to be both the users and
    a provider.

-   USERS & PROVIDER: These are "sub-entities." They hold specific
    details. For example, a PROVIDER has a skills attribute, while a
    USER has a home_address.

-   BOOKING: The central "transactional" entity. It brings a User and a
    Provider together for a specific date and service.

-   PAYMENT: Stores the financial result of a booking.

2. Key Relationships

-   User to Users/Provider (1:1): One User record corresponds to exactly
    one Users or Provider profile.

-   Users to Booking (1:M): One Users can make Many (M) bookings over
    time, but each booking belongs to only one user.

-   Provider to Booking (1:M): One Provider can be hired for Many jobs,
    but each specific booking instance is assigned to one provider.

-   Booking to Payment (1:1): In this model, each booking generates
    exactly one payment record.b

3. Primary Keys (PK) and Foreign Keys (FK)

-   Primary Key (PK): A unique identifier for every row (e.g., user_id).

-   Foreign Key (FK): A "link" to another table. For example, user_id
    inside the BOOKING table tells the database exactly which users made
    that request.

Relational Model Diagram

The Relational Data Model (RDM), also known as the Physical Database
Schema, is the most technical version of your data design. While the ERD
focuses on the "concepts," the Relational Model focuses on the
implementation.

It details exactly how the tables will look in a database management
system (like MySQL or PostgreSQL), including data types and key
constraints.

1. Data Types

In this model, we specify how each piece of data is stored:

-   INT (Integer): Used for IDs (e.g., user_id).

-   VARCHAR (Variable Character): Used for text like names and emails.

-   DECIMAL: Used for money (e.g., hourly_rate) to ensure precision.

-   DATETIME: Used to record exactly when a job takes place.

-   BOOL (Boolean): Used for simple Yes/No fields like is verified.

2. Integrity Constraints

-   Primary Key (PK): A unique identifier for every row. No two users
    can have the same user_id.

-   Foreign Key (FK): These are the "bridges" between tables. For
    example, the user_id in the BOOKINGS table is a Foreign Key that
    points back to the USER table.

-   Unique (UQ): Applied to fields like email so that two people cannot
    sign up with the same address.

3. Table Relationships

-   The "User" Split: To keep data clean, the USERS table holds common
    info (login), while Users and providers hold specialized info. They
    are linked via a user_id FK.

-   The Transaction Link: The BOOKINGS table acts as a junction, linking
    a user and a provider. The payments table then links to a specific
    booking, ensuring every cent is accounted for.
